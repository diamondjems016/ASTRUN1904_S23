# ASTRUN1904_S23
Teaching materials for Astronomy Lab II at Columbia University

This repository contains materials for ASTUN1904, a non-major astronomy lab at Columbia University, that was taught in Spring 2023. Labs were run once per week for 3 hours at night. There is intended to be minimal to no work outside of the lab. This lab focused on phenomena beyond the Solar System, including but not limited to exoplanets, dark matter, stellar spectroscopy, and Hubble expansion. The particular section of the lab was co-taught with Ryan Golant and had 11 students. The first time I taught the lab was in Spring 2022 (materials in public repo ASTRUN1904_S22).  This repository contains labs that reflect the feedback received in Spring 2022.

Syllabus: The first section is devoted to the class overview, which makes clear the learning objectives of the class as well as a quick breakdown of the class structure. Doing this makes the class expectations easy to find for the students. This is followed by details on the materials that students are expected to bring to class. This helps to ensure that students are at least materially prepared to participate in the class. We also are sure to make clear the materials that may be difficult financially for students to obtain (i.e. laptops) are available to them for the class. The grading breakdown sets clear expectations for how students should expect to be evaluated in the class. Of particular note is the participation section. In the past, the participation section was, for lack of a better description, a fudge factor, for which there were no clear guidelines for how to do well beyond “ask questions and participate in class”. In this updated syllabus, multiple avenues for engagement with the class were provided to ensure that students were able to participate in a way they were comfortable with. Finally, we included policies for attendance, accommodations, and academic honesty to make clear to students what would and would not be tolerated in the class, and what steps they need to take for exceptions to the policies listed in the document.

Labs: Labs for this course was designed by myself and Ryan Golant. Each lab (1-10) is intended to be a self-contained lesson and exploration of that week's concept. While the lab nominally touches on topics relevant to several astronomy lecture classes that link to the lab, since there are multiple lecture classes that link to the lab, it is not an exact match with respect to topics or timing of topics. These labs were designed with the intention of having students explore each topic using several modes of engagement including interactive web applications, simulations, data, interacting with domain experts, and physical modeling. Each lab ends with the same section: a space for students to voice remaining questions, and a space for students to provide feedback on the lab.

Final Project: For this iteration of the course, the final project was issued as a (optionally) creative presentation on a topic of their choice. Giving students agency over the topic as well as the mode of presentation allowed students not only to connect astronomy to their own backgrounds, but also encouraged deeper thinking about public science communication and connections between science and other interdisciplinary topics. Students were encouraged to choose a topic that had not been covered in class at all, and were provided guidance about major questions they might consider answering about their chosen topic. The rubric was provided upfront so that students could reference it as they crafted their presentations. This rubric is the same as the one used in the Chambliss competition run at each meeting of the American Astronomical Society.
